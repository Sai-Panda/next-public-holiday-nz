# Waitangi Day colour guidance

**Status:** research and provenance record for the current Waitangi Day treatment.
**Reviewed:** 5 September 2026  
**Scope:** a respectful Waitangi Day theme for the Aotearoa New Zealand public-holiday countdown.

## Short answer

There is **no official Waitangi Day colour palette**. Do not present any palette as “the colours of Waitangi Day”. Waitangi Day is New Zealand’s national day and marks the signing of Te Tiriti o Waitangi / the Treaty of Waitangi on 6 February 1840. [Manatū Taonga: *Waitangi Day*](https://nzhistory.govt.nz/politics/treaty/waitangi-day)

The strongest source-backed routes are flag colours that have an explicitly documented relationship to the day, or quiet documentary/site materials. They require careful framing: colour can refer to a flag or a physical setting; it should not claim to represent all Māori, Te Tiriti, or Waitangi Day itself.

## Hero image provenance and rights

Flickr identifies the work as **CC BY-NC-ND 2.0**; retain the source credit and [license link](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/2.0/) wherever the asset is published. This repository uses the unaltered scan as a documentary hero image, with no text or graphics over the document itself.

## Evidence-backed colour references

| Reference | Supported colours | What the source actually supports | Design implication |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Tino Rangatiratanga flag** | Black, red, white | Manatū Taonga describes black at the top, red at the bottom and a white centre. Cabinet recognised it as the *preferred national Māori flag*, but it does **not** have official status. The Ministry says flying it with the New Zealand flag on nationally significant days such as Waitangi Day symbolises and enhances the Crown–Māori relationship. [Manatū Taonga](https://www.mch.govt.nz/our-work/flags-anthems-and-emblems/new-zealand-flag/tino-rangatiratanga-flag) | A restrained black/red/off-white palette is defensible **when explicitly framed as a reference to this flag**. Do not describe it as the official Waitangi Day palette, reproduce its layout/koru, or invent “Māori-inspired” patterning. |
| **Te Kara / United Tribes flag** | Red, white, blue | At Waitangi Treaty Grounds, Te Kara is one of the three historic flags flown at the flagstaff. It was selected by chiefs at Waitangi in 1834. [Waitangi Treaty Grounds: *Flagstaff*](https://www.waitangi.org.nz/visit/flagstaff) | A red/white/blue route is an appropriate **historic Waitangi-site** reference, not a general Māori palette or official holiday identity. Use a documentary/flagstaff reading, not a generic patriotic treatment. |
| **New Zealand flag** | Official digital red `#C91235`, blue `#02216E`, white | Manatū Taonga gives these official reproduction values. The flag is a government flag-day reference on 6 February, but its colours are not designated colours of the holiday. [Manatū Taonga: *New Zealand flag*](https://www.mch.govt.nz/our-work/flags-anthems-and-emblems/new-zealand-flag) | If national-flag colours are used, treat them as a secondary historical/national reference; avoid making the page feel like a generic sporting or patriotic campaign. |
| **Te Tiriti document material** | Aged paper, brown-black ink | Archives New Zealand records the sheets as paper/parchment with ink ranging from light brown to dark black. [Archives NZ: *The sheets and signatures of Te Tiriti o Waitangi*](https://www.archives.govt.nz/discover-our-stories/the-treaty-of-waitangi/the-sheets-and-signatures-of-te-tiriti-o-waitangi) | Warm parchment, charcoal ink and a dark inky accent are an understated, non-flag route. Do not use a document scan or signatures as decoration without checking rights, context and legibility. |

## Recommended safe palette direction

For the existing dark-ink/cream editorial system, use a **documentary, photo-led theme**:

- Base: existing warm cream and deep ink (avoid assigning cultural meaning to them).
- Accent: a single muted Treaty-red, derived from the Tino Rangatiratanga or Te Kara flag context, used sparingly for dates/rules/buttons rather than as a claim of official identity.
- Supporting neutral: parchment/timber brown only if it comes from the user-supplied, licensed photograph or document-material reference.
- Hero: the requested real photograph should carry the site-specific character. A photo of a permitted Waitangi setting, landscape, flagstaff, or event is more grounded than adding a generic motif.

A starting UI palette—not an authoritative cultural palette—is:

`ink #1D211C` · `cream #F6F0E5` · `treaty red #9F2734` · `parchment #D8C7A9`

Before production, validate the photo’s licence/provenance and crop, obtain Māori cultural/design review for any explicitly Māori visual reference, and test final text contrast against the photo treatment.

## Guardrails

- Do not call black/red/white “Waitangi Day colours”; call them a reference to the Tino Rangatiratanga flag only when that context is present.
- Do not recreate, trace, crop into, or use the Tino Rangatiratanga flag/koru as a decorative device. The flag’s forms and meanings are specific.
- Do not invent kōwhaiwhai, whakairo, tukutuku, tā moko, or “Māori-inspired” graphics. Waitangi Treaty Grounds notes that Te Whare Rūnanga brings together distinct carving styles from iwi across Aotearoa, which is precisely why a generic treatment is inappropriate. [Waitangi Treaty Grounds: *Te Whare Rūnanga*](https://www.waitangi.org.nz/visit/te-whare-runanga)
- Avoid a uniformly celebratory national-brand tone. The Ministry’s history describes Waitangi Day as a day of reflection on the Treaty and notes enduring tensions around its commemoration. [Manatū Taonga](https://nzhistory.govt.nz/politics/treaty/waitangi-day)
- Use the user’s licensed, human-created photograph rather than generated imagery, consistent with the repository’s asset rule. Ask an appropriate Māori creative or cultural adviser to review any treatment that goes beyond neutral editorial colour and photography.

## Recommendation

Choose a restrained **ink / cream / muted red** theme with the licensed photo as the hero. It stays recognisably connected to a documented Waitangi Day flag context without claiming an official colour scheme or appropriating cultural visual language. Keep any copy factual: “Waitangi Day · 6 February” is safer than slogans or broad claims about what the palette represents.
